Holiday Visit Bathroom Check: 12 Signs at Mom or Dad's

Fifteen minutes in their bathroom can tell you what a phone call never will.

What should you look for in a parent's bathroom over the holidays?

Watch the movements, not the fixtures. If your parent grabs a towel bar to climb out of the tub, pushes off the sink to stand from the toilet, or has quietly stopped bathing, the bathroom no longer fits them. In the CDC's national data, 81.1% of bathroom injuries were caused by falls.

The holidays are often the first time in months that grown children see a parent's daily routine up close. You are in the house overnight, you hear the shower run (or not), and you notice the small workarounds people build when something gets hard. Those workarounds are the signal. A parent who says "I'm fine" on the phone may be holding the sink with both hands in person.

The CDC found that 68.3% of nonfatal bathroom injuries happened in or near the tub or shower, and another 14.1% involved standing up from, sitting down on, or using the toilet. Those are the two moments to watch. The injury rate also climbs steeply with age, which is why something that was fine at 70 can be risky at 82.

How do you run a 15-minute bathroom walk-through?

1. Start at the bed, not the bathroom

Walk the route your parent takes at 2 a.m. Count the light switches they would have to find, the rugs they would cross and the door thresholds they would step over. Night trips are when people are least steady and least likely to turn on a light.

2. Stand where they stand

Step into the tub (dry) and step out again. Notice what your own hands reach for. If the only thing within reach is a towel bar, a soap dish or the shower curtain rod, those are what your parent is grabbing too, and none of them are built to hold body weight.

3. Check the water and the reach

Run the hot tap for a minute and feel it carefully. If it is too hot to keep your hand under, the water heater is likely set above the 120 degrees F that the Consumer Product Safety Commission recommends. Then look at where the soap, shampoo and towels live. Anything on the floor of the tub or above shoulder height means bending or reaching while wet.

4. Sit on the toilet and stand up without your hands

If that is hard for you, it is harder for them. A grab bar beside the toilet or a toilet safety frame is one of the most effective small fixes.

5. Look for what is missing

No bath mat inside the tub, no night light, no phone within reach of the bathroom. Write it down and finish the visit before you start fixing things.

How do you bring it up without a fight?

Most older adults hear "bathroom safety" as "you are getting old," so lead with what they want: staying in their own home. AARP's 2024 survey found 75% of adults 50 and older want to stay in their current home as they age. A grab bar is how that happens.

  • Pick a calm moment, not right after a stumble or in front of the grandkids.
  • Describe what you saw ("I noticed you held the towel bar getting out"), not what you fear.
  • Offer one small fix first and do it together that weekend.
  • If a bigger change may be needed, suggest a no-pressure look by a professional so it is not just your opinion against theirs.

When is it time for more than grab bars?

Grab bars, a bench and a handheld shower solve a lot. They do not lower the tub wall. If your parent now uses a walker or wheelchair, cannot step over the tub wall safely even with a bar, or has stopped bathing out of fear, the next step is a lower-entry shower. Aging Safely Baths publishes $13,500 to $17,000 for a roll-in or handicap shower fully installed after current promotions, usually done in one day, and $15,250 to $20,000 for a walk-in tub. Quotes are itemized and written, by phone or from photos, and a factory-certified tech does a full site visit before install.

Ask about funding before anyone signs. Original Medicare generally does not pay for bathroom changes, but some Medicare Advantage plans, Medicaid waivers and the VA HISA grant (up to $6,800 for qualifying veterans) may help. No bathing product eliminates the risk of falls; products are designed to reduce risk when installed and used properly.

Holiday visit questions

What should I check in my parent's bathroom during a holiday visit?

Watch how they get in and out of the tub, how they stand up from the toilet, and what they grab for balance. Check for a towel bar used as a handhold, loose rugs, a dark path at night, very hot water and signs they have stopped bathing. Those tell you more than the fixtures do.

How do I know if my parent is afraid to shower?

Look for a dry tub and dry towels, a strong body odor, unwashed hair, or a parent who says they bathe at the sink. Many older adults quietly stop using a tub after a near-fall. Ask about it directly and calmly, without blame.

What is the cheapest bathroom safety fix that helps the most?

Removing loose rugs, adding night lights from the bedroom to the toilet, and turning the water heater to 120 degrees F cost little or nothing. A grab bar screwed into framing at the tub entry is the next step for most families.

When is it time to replace the tub with a walk-in shower?

When your parent cannot step over the tub wall safely even with a grab bar and a bench, has stopped bathing out of fear, or now uses a walker or wheelchair. Aging Safely Baths publishes $13,500 to $17,000 for a roll-in or handicap shower fully installed.

Does Medicare pay for grab bars or a walk-in shower?

Original Medicare generally does not. Some Medicare Advantage plans include bathroom safety benefits, Medicaid waivers may pay for home modifications, and eligible veterans may get the VA HISA grant of up to $6,800. Check each program before you buy.
